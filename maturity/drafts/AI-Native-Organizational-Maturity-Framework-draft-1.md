If you're feeling lost or wondering how your organization thinks it's "doing AI," use this framework to understand the problems and drive change.

In my past life as a UX designer I spent years preaching **Human-Centered Design (HCD)**, and it wasn’t until Norman Nielsen’s 6-stages of UX maturity did I understand how to diagnose the problem. Now, as companies chase AI initiatives, the same predictable patterns are emerging. Here are the 6-stages of AI maturity and some common traps you’ll run into along the way.   

**Stage 1 \- Resistant (Can we trust AI?)** 

**"Prove it's safe first."** AI is treated as suspicious, off-policy, or mostly a compliance risk. People use AI quietly, if at all.  Murmurs more around risk rather than value.  No sanctioned tools, data boundaries, or guidance.  AI work is framed as cheating, lazy, or low-quality output.  AI is in presentations, but not in the budget. 

* **🤔 Strategy:** AI isn't in the operating plan.  
* **🤷 Culture:** Fear, skepticism, and shadow usage are the norm.  
* **📋 Process:** Traditional workflows only.  
* **📉 Outcomes:** No AI-related metrics.

**Common failure mode:** Moral panic — fear-driven restrictions that prevent any learning.

**Stage 2 \- Opportunistic (AI is a Tool)** 

**"Let people experiment."** A few individuals use AI to go faster, but it is personal productivity, not team capability. Strong individual performers are prompting, coding with copilots, using research assistants, and streamlining meeting summaries.  Wins are anecdotal (“Look what I got Claude to do…”) No shared prompts, no shared context, AI just accelerates some tasks but doesn’t change how the work is done. 

* **🤔 Strategy:** AI is in individual toolbelts, not team plans.  
* **🤷 Culture:** Usage is optional, self-directed, and uneven.  
* **📋 Process:** Traditional processes with personal AI bolt-ons.  
* **📉 Outcomes:** Gains are anecdotal, not measured.

Common failure mode: This is classic **Hero Culture**. One person is 3x faster, but when they leave, the capability leaves with them. The mindset is personal productivity, not team capability.

🏃‍♂️Before you can move on, ask yourself what is driving change, is it FOMO or Innovation? FOMO: “Everyone else is doing AI. We need to be doing AI too.” vs Innovation: “We believe AI workflows will make us fundamentally better. We’re committing resources to find out how.” Only one of these will ever make it to later stages. 

**Stage 3 \- Assisted (AI makes us faster)** 

**"Where can AI make us faster?"** Teams standardize a few AI-supported workflows, but humans still do almost all orchestration. Common use cases are documented and templated: PRDs, summaries, prototyping, project-level defined CLAUDE.md or agent files. Shared knowledge in teams, templates, context libraries, and we’re seeing increases in velocity.  You’re here when you see tensions between AI-native team members and traditional workflows. 

* **🤔 Strategy:** Team-level AI adoption, not org-wide.  
* **🤷 Culture:** A team norm, but the broader org is only curious.  
* **📋 Process:** AI-augmented traditional processes with some shared patterns.  
* **📉 Outcomes:** Team velocity is up, but the organization's ability to absorb that output is lagging.

**Common failure mode:** Prompt theater;  people go through the motions of "using AI" but workflows haven't actually changed. AI is added onto processes rather than redesigning them.

💁‍♂️Stage 3 teams will start producing more work than the org can absorb. They’re bottlenecked not by velocity, but downstream processes (review cycles, approval chains, token limits) This creates the pressure that forces the commitment cliff. 

**Stage 4 \- Systematized (Our team uses AI)** 

**"Which workflows should be AI-assisted by default?"** AI is designed into core workflows, with governance, evaluation, and measurable business value. Teams are defining where AI is acceptable, unacceptable, and scaffolding HITL validation. Shared knowledge assets start to appear with cross-functional workflows (not just in engineering).  

* **🤔 Strategy:** AI is in the operating plan with explicit workflow designations.  
* **🤷 Culture:** Org-wide expectation, shared practices, and clear norms.  
* **📋 Process:** Defined flows, reusable patterns, QA gates, and escalation rules.  
* **📉 Outcomes:** Cycle time, throughput, and quality are actually being measured and reported.

**Common failure mode:** Bureaucracy without redesign. Governance layers are added but the underlying work structure doesn't change. AI gets slower because of process overhead, not faster.

**🛑 The AI Commitment Cliff.**   
Unlike NN/g’s UX maturity, getting beyond this point is a binary decision where you either become AI-native or you don't. The organization has to decide: **"AI-native workflows are how we work. Not optional. Not experimental.  This is the default."** If you don't commit, you’re left with hybrid teams that move at two different speeds that can’t collaborate.  

**Stage 5 — AI-First (Org redesign)** 

**"How should the team work if intelligence is abundant?"** The team no longer asks "Should we use AI here?" It designs work assuming AI collaborators are present. This requires **Organization Redesign**. Hiring changes and roles are redefined around judgment, taste, systems thinking, and exception handling. Documentation, playbooks, and internal knowledge are structured so AI can use them well.  Functional boundaries begin to soften and orgs flatten because AI handles much of the layers between disciplines. 

* **🤔 Strategy:** AI capability is a strategic asset in planning and headcount models.  
* **🤷 Culture:** AI-native is "how things are done here"—it's part of the company's identity.  
* **📋 Process:** Proprietary AI-native processes (not just augmented traditional ones).  
* **📉 Outcomes:** AI ROI is measured; cycle time is in days, not sprints.

**Common failure mode:** Speed without judgment \-  shipping faster but losing the human oversight that catches errors, considers customer trust, or strategic misalignment.

**Stage 6 \- AI-Native (Human-AI Collaborative Design driven Orgs)**

**"What should humans uniquely do?"** The operating model, team design, and product strategy are built around human-agent collaboration as a core capability.  The company is structured around outcomes, not functional silos, and AI systems own meaningful sub-workflows under human supervision. The company's products, internal processes, and strategy all assume AI as infrastructure. Competitive advantage comes from better orchestration, not just better code.

* **🤔 Strategy:** AI-native is a market differentiator; internal capabilities become external products.  
* **🤷 Culture:** Attracts talent based on this working model; organized by outcomes.  
* **📋 Process:** Agents own sub-workflows; frontier experimentation is standard practice.  
* **📉 Outcomes:** Planning, delivery, knowledge reuse, and decision support are deeply AI-mediated.
